### 1.2. Agenda

Choose a problem, pick greenfield or brownfield, and write a plan you get approved before building.

Phase 2: Build in ADK

Implement the agent, starting simple and adding tools, state, or more agents only as the problem demands.

### 1.3. The Brief

Do something agentic.

Build an AI agent in Google ADK that takes a real task off a real person’s plate. You pick the task, design the agent, and decide when one agent is enough and when the problem needs tools, state, or a team of agents.

### 1.4. The Story

You’re a forward-deployed engineer, embedded with a team that’s drowning in some repetitive task: triaging reports, checking releases, reconciling records, answering the same questions over and over. They want something that does the work and explains itself, not another dashboard.

You have an agent framework and three days of practice. Build the thing that proves an agent can carry real work, then show the team why they can trust it.

### 1.5. Why This Capstone Exists

You’ve spent three days building agents in Google ADK, with spec-driven planning and assistant-driven development before that. This capstone is where you put them together without a lab sheet walking you through each step.

The goal is narrow: show that you can develop agentic AI in a Google ADK environment, from a blank plan to a running, evaluated agent you can explain.

### 1.6. What Counts as Agentic

A single prompt and response isn’t agentic. Your build qualifies when the agent does more than answer:

  * It **decides** : it chooses what to do based on input and state, not a fixed script

  * It **acts** : it calls tools, reads or writes state, or coordinates other agents

  * It **produces structured output** a downstream system or person can use directly

### 1.7. Success Criteria

Your capstone succeeds when all of these hold:

# | Criterion  
---|---  
1 | The agent is built in Google ADK and runs  
2 | It’s genuinely agentic: it decides and acts through tools, state, or sub-agents  
3 | It started from a written description and plan you got approved before building  
4 | Its output is structured and validated, not free text  
5 | At least one evaluation or test shows it behaves the way you claim  
6 | You can explain your design decisions and trade-offs in the showcase  
  
Agree on these with your instructor at the approval gate. They are your rubric.

### 1.8. How the Capstone Runs

The capstone runs in four phases, and the first one gates the rest.

  1. **Define and plan.** Choose a problem, pick greenfield or brownfield, write a short description and plan.

  2. **Build.** Implement in ADK, starting simple and adding complexity only when the problem demands it.

  3. **Prove it works.** Evaluate or test the agent against the behavior you promised.

  4. **Showcase.** Demo it and walk through your decisions.

### 1.9. The Approval Gate

You don’t write agent code until your plan is approved.

Phase 1 produces a short document: the problem you’re solving, what the agent will do, what’s in and out of scope, and how you’ll know it works. You practiced this in AIC-2101 as the OpenSpec propose step. Here it’s the same discipline, sized to your project and reviewed by your instructor.

The gate exists so you commit to a clear target before ADK code pulls you somewhere you didn’t intend to go.

### 1.10. Greenfield or Brownfield

Your project starts in one of two conditions.

**Greenfield**

You start from nothing. You own every part: the agent, its tools, its data shapes, its output. Only your plan constrains you.

**Brownfield**

You integrate with a system that already exists. You build alongside it without changing it.

### 1.11. The Brownfield Black-Box Rule

Treat an existing system as a **black box**. You build around it, never inside it.

  * You **may** mock it, so you can develop and test without the real system running

  * You **may not** change what’s inside it, or change the output it gives you

  * You **may** transform its output after you receive it: reshape it, enrich it, flag problems in it

Your agent reads what the black box produces and adds value on top. The black box stays exactly as it is.

### 1.12. Two Sample Projects

The lab guide includes one greenfield sample and one brownfield sample, each worked out in enough detail to build.

__Warning

These are samples, not assignments. You are not required to use either one. A problem you care about will almost always produce better work than one handed to you.

#### 1.12.1. Sample: Field Report Triage (Greenfield)

An operations team gets free-text field reports. The agent reads each one, classifies it, extracts structured details, and returns a prioritized action list.

#### 1.12.2. Sample: Pricing Quote Wrapper (Brownfield)

An existing pricing engine returns raw quotes you can’t change. The agent calls it as a black box, then normalizes, explains, and flags anomalies in the quotes before a person sees them.

### 2.2. What You Built

You scoped an agentic problem, got a plan approved, and built a working agent in Google ADK. You constrained its output with a schema, gave it the tools and state it needed, and proved its behavior with an evaluation set or a test suite.

No two groups built the same agent. Problems, constraints, and scope all varied, and that variety is the point: nobody handed you the steps, and working without them is what this capstone set out to measure.

### 2.3. What Agentic Work Demanded

Three decisions shaped your build:

  * **Where to draw the line.** You decided what the agent owns and what it leaves alone. For brownfield projects, that line was a black box you integrated with but never changed.

  * **How far to escalate.** You started with one agent and added tools, workflows, or sub-agents only when the problem called for them.

  * **How to prove it.** You turned "it works" into evidence: cases the agent handles, and a failure it handles gracefully.

### 2.4. Reflection

Discuss as a group:

  * When did writing the plan first save you from a wrong turn in the build?

  * Which success criterion was hardest to meet, and why?

  * If your agent ran in production tomorrow, what’s the first thing that would break?

  * For brownfield groups: what did treating the existing system as a black box cost you, and what did it protect?

### 2.5. Where This Goes Next

The arc you ran here, plan to agent to evidence, is the same one a forward-deployed engineer runs on a customer’s problem. The agent gets larger, the stakes get higher, and the evaluation matters more.
