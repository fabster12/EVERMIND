## Quality Driven Management

> **Sidebar — QDM Mission Statement:** QDM is how we work to deliver the Purple Promise. By using QDM, we engage our entire workforce in delivering market-leading customer experience and superior business performance.

Quality has always been a hallmark of FedEx. But FedEx never stands still — and neither do our customers. As our customers' needs and expectations evolve, we must continuously improve and innovate to meet them.

Our answer to this challenge is Quality Driven Management, or QDM for short. Developed by FedEx, built by FedEx and optimized for FedEx, QDM is an approach to continuous improvement and innovation that's as unique as FedEx itself.

QDM is fundamental to the way we work at FedEx. Everyone — at every level — can work smarter and achieve more by understanding and applying QDM.

### Fundamentals of QDM

**What Is QDM?**

QDM is how we work to deliver the Purple Promise. QDM is both a methodology and a philosophy — a set of principles, tools and processes that we use to drive continuous improvement and innovation, and a way of thinking about quality in everything we do. By providing a common language for quality, QDM helps us collaborate on improving performance across our entire enterprise — from the executive suite to the frontline, at every OpCo in every corner of the globe.

**What Are QDM's Goals?**

QDM helps us optimize our business processes to meet three critical goals:

1. Delivering the superior experiences our customers expect.
2. Delivering superior business performance by minimizing unnecessary effort, time and cost.
3. Engaging and empowering our entire workforce.

Note that these three goals go hand in hand. By improving the customer experience, we build the customer loyalty that fuels future growth. By optimizing our performance, we improve our service to customers as well as profitability. By engaging and empowering our entire workforce, we build a quality culture that sets the stage for future innovation.

> **Sidebar — quote:** "QDM gives us common ways to improve quality across our entire enterprise. It provides common language, tools, and methods that we all use to analyze the causes of problems and plan corrective actions. QDM is the way FedEx manages our business to drive quality and brand loyalty to new heights."
> — Fred Smith, Chairman, President and CEO, FedEx Corporation

**Why QDM?**

Isn't our quality good enough? After all, FedEx is already known the world over for superior quality.

The answer is no. In the competitive, fast-changing business we're in, standing still is a recipe for failure.

To understand why, let's look at some simple math. Assume we deliver 10 million shipments every day for 6 million customers. Now imagine that we are 99.9% perfect in meeting those customers' requirements — 99.9% perfect sounds great, right? But look closer and you'll see it's not.

Being 99.9% perfect means we would disappoint 10,000 FedEx customers every day. 10,000 is a lot of customers! In fact, in just 1,000 working days, we would risk disappointing every one of our customers — and at least some of those failures would cost us business.

Now, what if we could raise our performance level from 99.9% to 99.99%. In that case, we would disappoint each of our customers, on average, only once in 27 years. Just think of the enormous business impact that would make. If we disappoint customers less often:

- We have fewer shipment claims and damages to pay.
- We spend less time on resolving problems and "fire drills," and more time on constructive opportunities to improve and innovate.
- We have fewer lost customers to replace through marketing.

This simple math illustrates a profound truth. Quality isn't just one aspect of our business. Quality in many ways is our business. Quality drives superior customer experiences. Quality drives efficiency. Quality drives brand loyalty and revenue.

**When QDM?**

When do we apply QDM? Always. QDM isn't an emergency device we take out when a quality problem happens. QDM is how we build quality into the way we work every day.

### PSP, QDM and the Purple Promise

How do PSP and the Purple Promise relate to QDM? Together, these three concepts express a way of working and managing our business that's unique to FedEx:

- **How We Think: PSP** shapes our environment, and tells us three key priorities we must balance:
  - **People** first, our most valuable resource.
  - **Service** that exceeds expectations.
  - **Profit** to reward our people and fund our future.
- **How We Work: QDM** helps us work at our best — to generate continuous improvement and innovation in:
  - Delivering the **superior service** customers expect.
  - Delivering **superior business performance** by eliminating waste and inefficiency.
  - **Engaging and empowering** our workforce.
- **What We Deliver: The Purple Promise** sets the standard of performance we must consistently strive to achieve — individually and as an organization: *"I will make every FedEx experience outstanding."*

PSP empowers people to pursue excellence in the service we deliver to our internal and external customers. Quality involves teamwork, and everyone, at every level, has a vital role to play in FedEx quality. Engaging everyone's skills and talents helps us work smarter and create better ideas.

QDM principles, tools and methods help people generate new ideas for improving the way we work. QDM keeps us focused on ideas, big and small, to continuously improve performance and deliver better service to customers and to each other, time after time. QDM's focus on delivering service that exceeds customer expectations builds customer loyalty. Customer loyalty, along with greater efficiency, drives profit and fuels growth for our people and our organization.

QDM teaches us to speak up when we see a problem and address it in a blame-free way (view failures as opportunities). Problems guide us to what we need to improve. QDM focuses us on facts and data — not guesses — as we search for root causes (be scientific). Facts help us challenge assumptions and create more effective solutions. We measure so we can improve — we monitor the "critical few," the metrics that reflect what matters most, in order to make continuous improvement (measure, measure, measure).

QDM tells us that we don't define quality — our customers do. QDM tools keep us focused on delivering what customers require and expect (customers define quality). By optimizing our services to meet what customers expect, we optimize our operations to eliminate waste and inefficiency — and deliver customers greater value (optimize business performance).

## Components of QDM

> **Sidebar:** Just because Quality Driven Management includes the word "Management," don't think for a minute it's only for managers. QDM is about how everyone should manage their work — from executives to staff members, facilities managers to frontline personnel — and it can help everyone find ways to do their work better.

QDM consists of three major components:

- **Principles** — Fundamental quality concepts that form the foundation of QDM.
- **Tools** — Methods and techniques that help us analyze quality issues and opportunities in order to find new ways to improve and innovate.
- **Processes** — Including ABLE, a systematic, flexible approach to improving a business process or creating an innovation.

These components form a common language that helps us collaborate to build quality solutions and share best practices across business units.

## Principles of QDM

QDM is built on six guiding principles. These principles are simple, yet extremely powerful — because they focus us on what really matters for improving quality.

- Customers define quality.
- Be scientific.
- Measure, measure, measure.
- Optimize business performance.
- Quality involves teamwork.
- View failures as opportunities.

### Principle: Customers Define Quality

Let's begin with a definition that lays the groundwork for everything that follows — QDM's definition of quality itself.

> Quality is a measure of how well we meet customer requirements and expectations over time, including customer expectations of value.

As this definition shows, the starting point for quality is understanding that customers define quality — not us. QDM requires us to see ourselves through the eyes of our customers. It demands that we look at our performance from the outside in.

**Customers Can Be Internal or External**

Everyone at FedEx has two kinds of customers: internal and external.

An **internal customer** is the team or person who receives the output — or result — of your work. To identify your internal customers, look downstream. Those who receive the output of your work are your internal customers.

An **external customer** is someone who purchases a service or product from FedEx. The role of everyone at FedEx connects in some way to the needs of an external FedEx customer. That's because the overall FedEx customer experience depends on many more activities than shipping alone.

> **Sidebar — Customer Corridor Map:** Departments such as IT, accounting and legal sometimes struggle to see how their work connects to the needs of external FedEx customers. The FedEx customer corridor map can help. It shows how key stages in the customer relationship (becoming a customer → preparing to ship → shipping → resolving problems → maintaining relationship) connect to activities served by functional areas of FedEx (initiate and select provider, evaluate and stabilize account, set-up account, prepare to ship, ship, receive, resolve problems, pay, maintain account).

**Understand Customer Requirements and Expectations**

Because customers define quality, we must listen carefully to what customers tell us:

- When customers say things like "I want it there by tomorrow," they are specifying a level of service. This is a **requirement**.
- When customers say things like "I want to have confidence my package will get there on time," they are telling us a subjective benefit they want from dealing with FedEx. This is an **expectation**.

QDM asks that we work to deliver customer requirements and expectations — both the things that are easy to define, like on-time delivery, and ones that are more challenging to nail down, like confidence and respect.

Why both? The mission of QDM is to deliver the Purple Promise — to make every FedEx experience outstanding. To deliver on that promise, attributes like confidence, trustworthiness and friendliness are just as important as delivering shipments on time. What's more, when we empathize with customers and understand their expectations, we also gain insight into how their needs are evolving. This helps us keep in step with changing customer needs.

### Principle: Be Scientific

In a complex business like ours, the answers to quality issues are often not what they seem to be at first glance. So, QDM asks that we think like scientists — by proceeding systematically and considering facts first.

**Base Decisions on Facts and Data**

Jumping to a conclusion based on incomplete data or faulty assumptions is a major reason that projects fail. By gathering facts and information first, you can avoid many false starts and wrong turns. Facts and data will also help you build support for your ideas later — from both your team and your senior managers.

**Analyze Root Causes**

Root cause analysis is the process of considering all the facts, and working systematically through potential causes of a quality issue until the ultimate cause is uncovered. This ultimate cause is known in QDM as the **root cause**. Targeting the root cause will yield the most improvement.

Let's start with a simple example. A quality problem like late deliveries can have many potential causes — equipment breakdowns, bad weather, etc. Each of these may have dozens of other potential causes, like inadequate maintenance. In turn, poor maintenance might have a still deeper cause, like missing a service schedule — and so on.

Root cause analysis helps ensure that we identify and address the true, underlying causes of issues, instead of just applying band-aids to symptoms.

> **Sidebar — Root Cause Analysis / Five Whys example:**
> The root cause is the most fundamental reason for a quality problem. Correcting the right root cause will yield the greatest improvement. Five whys is a basic tool for getting beyond surface symptoms to underlying causes.
>
> - **Problem:** Help Desk Availability
> - **Why?** High call volumes at the Help Desk
> - **Why?** High number of repeat callers
> - **Why?** New Help Desk staff providing incorrect initial responses
> - **Why?** Staff provide responses based on memory
> - **Why? (root cause)** No standard responses are documented for staff to use

**Experiment to Find a Better Way**

> **Sidebar:** **Continuous improvement** — a philosophy of always looking for things that can be done better.

In addition to asking you to think like a scientist, QDM also asks you to innovate like one. After all, part of scientific thinking is experimenting — being willing to try new ideas, even if they fail.

There's a sound business reason for embracing innovation. No matter how good we are, there's always a higher level we can achieve. But to get there, we have to constantly look for and test new ideas for doing things better. In QDM, this idea is known as "continuous improvement."

To make continuous improvement, we have to be willing to open our minds to new ideas. To encourage innovation. To empower change.

### Principle: Measure, Measure, Measure

It's difficult, if not impossible, to improve what isn't measured. A measurement puts a stake in the ground. It gives us a reference point we can compare against, a way to track our progress over time.

But how do we measure something as abstract as quality? In QDM, there are three ways — by measuring failures, variation and success.

**Measuring Failures**

If we understand what customers require and expect, there is always something we can measure: Did we deliver what the customer wanted?

For example, the customer wanted the shipment there by 10:30 AM. Did our performance meet the customer requirement — or not? As this question shows, one way to measure quality is simply to count the number of failures.

> **Failure:** An occasion where we don't meet a customer requirement or expectation.

So, in order to make quality measurable, we find ways to correlate customer requirements, like "delivered by 10:30 AM," with things we can measure, like "late deliveries per thousand" or "errors per million."

Our goal in counting failures is not to dwell on the negative — it's to translate our performance into objective metrics we can track. By knowing where and when we fail, we discover how and where we can improve — and we can focus on the opportunities that will have the most impact.

**Measuring Variation**

Counting failures is often the most direct way to measure quality. Measuring variation is another way — one that gives a different kind of insight. Variation is a measure of how consistently we provide a positive customer experience.

> **Variation:** How much a measurement deviates from a goal over time.

To understand variation, let's look at the key difference between measuring variation and calculating an average. Suppose our target for on-time delivery is 99.0%. We take an average over 8 days, and find we're at 99.1%. We're better than our target! Time to celebrate? Not quite yet.

If we plot the data that led to our 99.1% average, we would see a graph, called a **run chart**, like the one below. As you can see, even though we met our target when averaged over eight days, on two of these days our performance was clearly far below our target. On those days, our customer reputation suffered. This highlights a key lesson:

> Measuring variation reflects the way customers actually experience our performance in a way that an average cannot.

Like it or not, the vivid memories created by one painful customer experience can wipe out a steady string of admirable performances. By reducing variation, we minimize the risk that "a bad day" will ruin lots of hard work — and cost us customers.

> **Sidebar — Run Charts example (On-time Delivery Percentage, goal 99%):**
>
> | Day | Value |
> |---|---|
> | 1 | 99.15% |
> | 2 | 99.3% |
> | 3 | 99.7% |
> | 4 | 99.6% |
> | 5 | 98.0% |
> | 6 | 99.4% |
> | 7 | 98.3% |
> | 8 | 99.4% |
> | **Avg.** | **99.1%** |
>
> Days 5 and 7 fall well below the 99% goal even though the average clears it — illustrating why variation matters more than the average alone.

**Measuring Success**

How do we find out whether our improvement efforts worked? We measure success by looking at the difference between our performance before and after implementation — with an eye toward understanding whether we truly delivered on our goals.

### Principle: Optimize Business Performance

Quality does not mean perfection at any cost. On the contrary, customers make real-world decisions based on the ratio of quality to price — or, in other words, value. To attract and retain customers, we must not only deliver what customers want, but also deliver it efficiently — by optimizing business performance.

**Focus on What Adds Value for Customers — and Eliminate What Doesn't**

When we truly understand customer requirements and expectations, we can use that information to determine the essential activities that add value for customers — and eliminate those that don't. This allows us to raise quality while at the same time lowering costs.

Here's an example. Suppose a work process is complicated and, as a result, an inspection is required to make sure the work has been done correctly. Now, what if we could re-design the process in such a way that mistakes would become nearly impossible? Or what if we could apply a new technology that would eliminate the most difficult steps? By "mistake-proofing" along these lines, we could:

- Streamline the process and save time — improving productivity.
- Eliminate steps — lowering costs.
- Reduce errors — improving quality.

As you see, optimizing the process is win-win. Work gets simpler and less prone to error. Costs go down. And customer experience improves.

**Get It Right the First Time: The 1-10-100 Rule**

A great way to improve efficiency is to "get it right the first time." When a task must be re-done or corrected later, the costs are large — in both time and money. In fact, experts say up to 20% of business expenses are due to mistakes.

> **Sidebar — The 1:10:100 Rule:** If it costs one dollar to do a job right, it will typically cost 10 times as much to go back and fix it later. And if we lose that customer due to the error, it will typically cost 100 times more to replace that customer with a new one.
>
> | Stage | Relative Cost |
> |---|---|
> | 1 — Prevention | $ |
> | 10 — Correction | $$ |
> | 100 — Failure | $$$ |

The 1:10:100 rule helps us understand why it's vital to do things right the first time.

### Principle: Quality Involves Teamwork

To do our own job well, we have to think about how our job relates to everyone else's. And how we all need to work together to succeed.

To understand why teamwork is so critical to quality, let's look at one of QDM's key concepts: the idea that work is a process.

**Work Is a Process**

When we look at our job in isolation, it's tough to see how important our role really is and how we contribute. That's why QDM encourages us to view our work as a vital part of a process — one in a series of steps that each add value to the final product.

> **Sidebar — example shipment delivery process:** Receive pickup request → Pick up shipment → Transport to facility → Load on plane or truck → Deliver to sorting facility → Sort → Load on plane or truck to destination → Unload at destination facility → Sort onto delivery vehicle → Deliver to recipient or retail counter.
>
> A process view of work helps us see the collaboration required to succeed. Even in this overview, you can glimpse the incredible coordination that FedEx operations require — air and ground operations, sorting operations, retail operations and so on. These groups also receive support from areas not shown in the diagram — information technology, invoicing, customer service and others.

This view of "work as a process" encourages us to step outside of the silo of our own department or team. It compels us to broaden our perspective, look beyond team, group and department boundaries, and see each role as a critical step in a long chain of activities.

Other teams and stakeholders up and down the line — as well as customers — are counting on us. To succeed, we must understand everyone's roles in the process and collaborate.

**Involve Everyone, Especially Those Close to the Work**

Who can tell you how to improve a process within your operations? The frontline managers and team members who do the job day in and day out are a great place to start.

In order to succeed, quality efforts have to involve everyone — from the frontline to the executive suite. In QDM, involving the whole team is not merely a way of making everyone feel important. It's a way of making sure we truly tap everyone's skills and ideas.

### Principle: View Failures as Opportunities

No real improvement can occur unless we openly acknowledge our failures. After all, if we don't know when and where our processes fail, we can't possibly know how to fix them. And we can never improve.

**Speak Up When Problems Occur**

This principle requires us to encourage everyone to surface problems. Make sure all levels of our organization, especially the frontline, know that speaking up is welcomed and valued. Bringing quality issues to the surface helps us find new ways to improve.

**Create a Blame-free Environment**

> **Sidebar — Seek the Truth:** Acknowledging shortcomings, in addition to being a principle of QDM, also reflects Integrity, one of the FedEx Shared Values. Integrity compels us to seek the truth in any situation. A clear priority for managers is to create an environment in which the truth can be openly acknowledged and discussed.

Blame has no place in QDM, because it focuses on the most superficial level of an issue and discourages people from speaking up. When team members feel they can call attention to problems, issues come to light sooner. And solutions are developed sooner as well.

**Find the Opportunity in the Problem**

When a problem emerges, look for the opportunity within it. Be objective and focus on facts and data. Seek root causes. Find the solution that will keep this same problem from occurring again, or research to find a new process or technology that addresses the issue.

**View Quality as a Work in Progress**

In QDM, quality is not some static ideal where everything works smoothly — until a problem comes along and disrupts paradise. Instead, quality is a journey where no matter how well we perform, we still find new ways to do things better.

## Tools

Quality tools are practical techniques that are used to analyze, plan and implement quality improvements and innovations, and they play a role in almost every aspect of QDM. The QDM toolkit is a comprehensive set of **more than 60 tools** specially selected for the FedEx environment. Visit the QDM website for more information and interactive tool finders that can guide you to the right tool.

> **Sidebar — Interactive Tool Finders:** Tool finders on the QDM website make it easy to find the right tool for the task at hand: root cause analysis; efficiency and cost improvement; customer experience; project management; quality analysis.

A small sampling of tools from the toolkit:

- **Run Chart** — A graph that depicts the degree of variation in a process over time. Used to help manage ongoing processes and to spot quality problems quickly.
- **Pareto Chart** — Quickly highlights the causes that are responsible for the majority of quality issues, so you can act first on those. Created by rearranging a bar chart so that it reads from the most frequently occurring category to the least (example: causes of customer complaints — call waiting time, no follow up, attitude/rudeness, wrong info, outages).
- **Fishbone (Cause and Effect) Diagram** — A way of visualizing possible causes by sorting them into categories (e.g. People, Environment, Process, Measurements, Inputs, Technology). The quality issue is written in the fish's "head," and potential causes are grouped into categories along the "bones."
- **Brainstorming** — A way of developing breakthrough ideas by involving everyone in problem-solving. Participants with different expertise or points of view join together to quickly generate ideas.
- **Multivoting** — Helps teams make choices. A structured system of team voting that can help teams narrow a long list of options to a few.
- **VOC Translation** (Voice of the Customer) — A way of translating customer expectations (e.g. "box looks good," "really speedy," "not delivered to neighbor") into translated customer requirements (damage free, timeliness, correct delivery) and then into quantifiable measurements/CTQs (% shipments undamaged, % on time, tracking accuracy, % required scans completed, % addresses valid, % airbills readable).
- **Flow Chart (Process Map)** — A drawing that shows the steps of a work process in sequence and creates a common understanding of how work flows. It's often helpful to compare the workflow on paper to that in reality.
- **Checklist** — Helps you organize work to make sure that no item is missed.
- **Mistake-proofing** — Designing processes so mistakes are prevented or less likely to occur, and failures that do occur are detected and corrected sooner. Simple example: using a dropdown menu of valid states instead of a free-text field.

## Overview of ABLE

How do we translate fundamental QDM concepts into real-world quality improvements? There are many ways, but one of the most important is by applying **ABLE**.

ABLE provides a simple, four-phase framework for making any type of quality improvement or innovation: **A**ssess, **B**uild, **L**aunch and **E**valuate.

- **Assess** the opportunity in order to establish a goal and a team.
- **Build** understanding of the opportunity, then build ideas for a solution.
- **Launch** the solution after careful planning.
- **Evaluate** to see if the solution really worked.

> **Sidebar:** The circular shape of the ABLE graphic conveys an important message: every improvement creates a new foundation for the next one. You should use the learning you gained from applying ABLE to generate ideas for future improvement and innovation.

### When to Use ABLE

ABLE can help you work through any problem or opportunity. It's designed to be:

- **Simple** — It can be used anywhere in our enterprise by anyone at any level.
- **Flexible** — It can tackle any kind of project.
- **Powerful** — It can be used for the most complex improvements and innovations as well as the simplest.

Because of this flexibility, you can use ABLE in many ways:

- **At your desk or in a team huddle** — Use the top-level phases of the ABLE process to think through and solve an issue quickly.
- **In a formal Quality Action Team (QAT)** — Use the ABLE Improvement Process to improve an existing business process.
- **To create a new product or process** — Use the Design-ABLE Process, which is optimized for creating innovations or new products.

> **Sidebar — Quality Action Team (QAT):** A group of people formally assigned to the task of improving a specific quality issue. A QAT is meant to be temporary — the team comes together to work intensively on an issue, then disbands once the problem has been solved.

## ABLE Improvement Process

The ABLE Improvement Process provides a detailed step-by-step method for making an improvement to an existing business process. Because there are many kinds of quality improvements, from simple to complex, not every project will require every step shown below.

### Assess Phase

ABLE begins with assessing the opportunity for improvement. In general, the first phase of ABLE has three parts:

1. Identifying an improvement opportunity.
2. Making a preliminary assessment by gathering some key facts and data about the opportunity.
3. Defining the goals and the team and, if necessary, starting a formal QAT.

**Identifying Improvement Opportunities**

Opportunities for improvement are all around you. Start brainstorming with team members in your work area and, within a half hour, you'll probably have a long list of ideas for potential improvements. Common categories of improvement ideas at FedEx:

- **Recurring failures.** When you see a problem occurring over and over again, it's a clue that there's a quality improvement waiting to be addressed.
- **Non-Value-Added.** Think about the QDM principle "Optimize Business Performance." Look for steps that don't add value or that can be eliminated because they're no longer needed.
- **Barriers.** Look for conditions or situations that prevent a process from meeting defined requirements. Examples might be inadequate instructions, required steps being omitted or a lengthy approval cycle.
- **Gaps.** Is there a gap between where our performance stands and where we need to be to meet the needs of internal or external customers?

> **Sidebar — ABLE Resources:** For additional resources that can help you learn and apply ABLE, visit the QDM website. There you'll find an ABLE A3 (one-page process flow diagram), an ABLE Gantt Chart, brief guides on applying ABLE to various business issues (improving business performance, reducing cost, improving customer experience, design and innovation), and more.

**Making a Preliminary Assessment: Gathering Key Facts and Data**

Although every issue is unique, three kinds of information are important to assessing almost every quality challenge:

- What is the current state of performance? And what is the impact?
- Who is involved? Remember the concept "Work is a Process" — though the issue may seem confined to your area, it could also impact those downstream from you, and perhaps even those upstream. Think through everyone who might be involved.
- What metrics might help? Are there existing metrics that might help with assessing this opportunity to improve?

**Defining Goals for the Opportunity**

It's easier to succeed with improvement ideas that are focused clearly. Two guidelines that can help you narrow your project's scope:

- **High leverage** — opportunities that will yield the most improvement the fastest.
- **Strategic fit** — opportunities that fit your unit's customer service or strategic goals.

Once you've focused, write an **opportunity statement** that defines the problem, describes its impact and sets a realistic goal for the improvement. This brief statement will serve as a starting point — one that you will refine as your project moves forward.

> **Sidebar — Opportunity Statement:** A clear and concise description of a quality problem, its extent and impact, and the benefits to be gained by solving it.
>
> *Sample Opportunity Statement*
> - **Current State:** 5% of shipments damaged
> - **Impact:** Dissatisfied customers and lost business; employee frustration; time/money on claims
> - **Desired State:** 99.8% of shipments delivered undamaged

### Build Phase

In the Build phase, you will:

- Build understanding of the issue and identify either the root cause or the opportunity.
- Build a solution and pilot it, if possible.
- Build the business case, if necessary, for implementing this improvement.

**Build Understanding**

The start of the Build phase is all about building your understanding of the improvement opportunity — by gathering more data and learning more. Avoid jumping to conclusions at this stage until you have more facts. Starting points:

- **What is the work process?** Understanding a quality issue is much easier if you have an accurate map of the process — a diagram of how the work really flows. Make sure to compare how the process works in reality versus how it's supposed to work on paper. Experience shows these can be very different.
- **What are the requirements and expectations?** Identify all those who participate in the process. Identify the teams upstream who give you input, and those downstream who receive the output of your process. Then work to understand their requirements and expectations.
- **What are potential causes?** Even a seemingly simple quality issue can have many potential causes. Now's the time to leave no stone unturned and consider all the possibilities.

Once a broad set of facts and data have been gathered, it's time to narrow the focus, and to start drilling deeper and deeper until the root cause has been identified.

By definition, the root cause is the cause that, once addressed, will yield the greatest improvement in quality. Visit the root cause tool finder on the QDM website to learn more about five whys, fishbone diagrams, Pareto charts and many others.

Once you've identified what you think is the right root cause, confirm that the data gathered supports your conclusions.

**Build a Solution**

Building a solution involves working from the negative — the problem and root cause you've identified — to the positive. "Here is the problem. We've figured out the root cause. We've gathered data to prove it. Now what are we going to do about it?"

- **Develop and prioritize potential solutions.** Chances are that several will seem worthwhile. The key is to choose the solution that will provide the greatest payback. The best options will make a process inherently easier, more efficient, more effective or more mistake-proof. Consider the complexity, cost-benefit ratio and risks of each.
- **Select a solution.** Tools such as multivoting can help a team reach consensus. Make sure to respectfully challenge the preferences expressed and to confirm that the data supports the choice of solution.
- **Pilot test.** The "Be Scientific" principle asks us to experiment. A pilot test is a limited-scale rollout designed to demonstrate whether or not a solution really works. Although not every solution requires a pilot, some kind of trial run is always desirable. Use pilot testing as a learning process, to help you iteratively refine your solution.

> **Sidebar — Be Careful of Checklist-driven Solutions:** While checklists are important quality tools, in general, a better solution is to make the process so clear and simple that it's mistake-proof. Mistake-proofing can eliminate the need for a checklist entirely.

**Build the Business Case**

Solutions that require major investment or large-scale changes in a work process may require additional review and approval by senior managers and executives before going forward.

Because each improvement effort is unique, the required approvals for every project will vary. If you are unclear about whose approval is required, consult with a QDM Expert or the Quality Lead at your OpCo for guidance.

If formal approval is required, the Business Review Guidelines tool, available on the QDM website, is a PowerPoint template that will help you capture your ABLE process in presentation form.

### Launch Phase

Improving inherently involves change. Since quality improvements often involve changing a business process, these changes can affect customers, internal as well as external.

For this reason, the Launch phase of ABLE is all about **planning first, before acting**. Key steps:

- Preparing an implementation plan that includes identifying those that your change will affect, and developing communication and training plans that will prepare everyone for implementation.
- Defining a control plan for monitoring the change, tracking progress and confirming whether the solution worked.
- Implementing the change once planning is complete.

**Preparing the Implementation Plan**

The goal of the implementation plan is to make sure you have thought through all the details, and that everyone who needs to know about the change is informed in advance and ready for the upcoming launch. An implementation plan should answer four questions:

- **What** — details of the process change that will be made.
- **Who** — all those who need to know about the change, and their roles. Develop a communication plan to ensure that these groups are both informed of the change and understand its impact.
- **How** — instructions or process flow diagrams. Also develop a training plan to help team members adequately prepare for the launch.
- **When** — the timeline for making each change.

If you did a pilot test, draw on the lessons learned to refine your Launch plan. Bear in mind that, in a full-scale implementation, the groups involved — and the impacts — may grow exponentially.

**Defining a Control Plan**

Defining a control plan involves:

- Deciding how you will quantitatively measure success — what metrics will you use and what degree of performance improvement is required.
- Choosing other performance metrics that will help you see whether this process change has any other impacts, positive or negative. These will help ensure that your proposed solution doesn't have unintended side effects.
- Creating an implementation dashboard or improvement scorecard that you will use to communicate status at a glance.

> **Sidebar — Dashboards and Scorecards:** Easy-to-read graphics that enable the status of key metrics to be seen at a glance (e.g. an implementation dashboard tracking accuracy before/after against a goal).

**Launch the Full-scale Implementation**

You are now ready to see your careful planning become reality. Do not hesitate. Quality experts have a saying: "Plan slowly. Implement quickly." You've worked hard for this moment. Now go out and make it happen.

### Evaluate Phase

Now that your improvement is underway, it's time to Evaluate — to track how well the solution is working. Key steps in this final phase of ABLE:

- Measure and post performance data.
- Evaluate the performance data:
  - If the solution achieves its goal, document it fully so that you can share the best practices you've learned.
  - If the solution doesn't reach its goal, return to an earlier phase of ABLE and try again.
- Reward and recognize all participants.

**Measure and Post Performance Data**

As your improvement project rolls out, measure and post results. Follow your control plan carefully, and make sure to share your results with everyone involved.

**Evaluate the Performance Data**

Analyze the full-scale data carefully, using the tools that you defined earlier in your control plan. See if things are turning out the way you envisioned.

Typically, you should evaluate your solution for at least **60 to 90 days** to ensure that results are sustainable. Additional time may be needed in some instances.

If goals are achieved, document the solution. Sharing your project's details, its results and the thinking behind it will enable others to learn from your efforts. This supports several goals of QDM:

- Establishing best practices that can cascade throughout the organization and inspire other improvements.
- Improving business performance by reducing duplication of effort.
- Making quality efforts visible across the enterprise.

What if your goals are not being achieved? A famous quote about quality science says: "Quality is a learning process." Through your efforts, you've undoubtedly learned a lot about the process you're trying to improve. Based on what you know now, you may see that an adjustment — or even an entirely different solution — is called for. If so, armed with the wealth of new knowledge you've gained, return to an earlier phase of ABLE and try again.

**Reward and Recognize All Participants**

Once your goal has been achieved, don't neglect to celebrate! Celebrating wins is vital. By recognizing everyone who contributed to the improvement effort, we build momentum for future improvements. Finally, take some time to consider what other opportunities cropped up while you were working on this one — it's time to start thinking about that next improvement!

## Design-ABLE Process

The Design-ABLE Process is a variation of ABLE that is optimized for:

- Creating an entirely new product, service or business process.
- Exploring the potential of new or emerging technologies.

Design-ABLE is targeted at projects that go beyond improving an existing process. Design-ABLE focuses on:

- Developing empathy for what customers experience as they interact with our products, services and processes.
- Generating creative solutions that stem from a deep understanding of customer needs and behaviors.
- Testing the potential of ideas quickly through an iterative approach in order to encourage small-scale risk-taking and rapid learning.

> **Sidebar:** The Design-ABLE Process can be used to create small, incremental innovations or game-changing ones.

**Design-ABLE Process steps:**

1. Assess opportunities to delight the customer and improve business performance — and frame the design challenge.
2. Build understanding through research, synthesize new insights, and design the solution through iterative prototyping and testing.
3. Launch the minimally viable solution on a limited scale, through careful planning and control.
4. Evaluate the implementation to improve and enhance the solution.

To learn more, visit the QDM website or the Customer Driven Design website (keyword: CDD).

## Applying QDM

> **Sidebar:** Frontline QDM Best Practices Picture Book — ways to apply QDM in frontline operations.

QDM is about making improvements and innovations of all kinds, of all sizes. You should apply QDM not only to big projects, but also to how you approach your work every day. Apply QDM:

- **As a guiding philosophy.** QDM is foundational to the FedEx culture. It works together with PSP and the Purple Promise to help us deliver superior customer experiences and business success.
- **As a management system.** In managing your own work or the work of others, you should monitor and analyze SQI and local KPI metrics in order to assess performance and identify quality issues that need attention.
- **As a continuous improvement methodology.** Apply QDM principles, tools and methods such as ABLE in an integrated, coordinated way to make continuous improvement in a business process.
- **To encourage innovation.** Use QDM principles and methods — especially Design-ABLE — to identify emerging customer needs, spark innovation and manage change in your work area.

### Frontline QDM

At FedEx, quality is not solely the job of one special department. Quality is everyone's job. That's why Frontline QDM is a key component of QDM.

Frontline QDM recognizes that, to succeed, we must tap into the best ideas of everyone — especially those closest to the work.

A variety of resources have been created to help managers and teams apply QDM at the frontline, such as the Frontline QDM Best Practices Picture Book, a QDM Wall of Fame board and award template, a Frontline QDM messages playbook and more. Access them on the QDM website.

### QDM for Monitoring and Corrective Action

The best way to apply QDM isn't to wait for a problem to come along. The best way is to find simple, effective ways to integrate QDM into your daily work.

- **Monitor.** Monitor the quality of your work using QDM tools, and record and analyze the results over time with an eye toward improvement.
- **Surface problems.** A growing number of errors or exceptions, a string of delayed items, or a run chart with a downward trend are all signs that something has changed in your business process. Surfacing these problems — and encouraging every team member to do so — is essential to making sure that quality issues are constructively investigated and solved.
- **Act.** Once you've identified a problem, take corrective action. In the fast-paced, time-critical FedEx environment, some problems need to be solved fast — especially when the issues concern safety or customer experience. In these cases, use common sense and don't agonize too much over procedures or processes. A quick huddle with team members to talk over the problem using QDM principles can help. For a more complicated issue, teams and individuals often find it helpful to walk quickly through ABLE at the top-most level — Assess the problem, Build a solution, Launch, then Evaluate. If the first solution doesn't work, don't hesitate to try another. The important thing is to act.

Once the immediate problem is taken care of, then go back and tackle it in a deeper, more comprehensive manner using the QDM principles, tools and especially ABLE.

### Leadership in Applying QDM

QDM is about creating change — and change always requires superior leadership. Some QDM best practices that leaders can apply:

- **Understand why QDM is important and communicate it.** To lead QDM efforts, communicate not only the how of QDM but also the why. And the why is this: QDM enables us to consistently deliver the Purple Promise, which builds brand loyalty among customers. Brand loyalty drives growth in our business that benefits us all.
- **Lead by example.** To encourage others around you to apply QDM, apply QDM yourself. Model the QDM behaviors you want from your team. Ask yourself: Do I base my decisions and actions on facts and objective data? Do I put customers first? Do I explore issues collaboratively and seek out the input of all those involved?
- **Empower everyone.** The most effective ideas for improving quality often come from those closest to the work. By tapping the insights of those who do the work every day, we leverage everyone's best ideas and talents.
- **Create a blameless culture.** In order to make QDM work, team members must feel they can bring quality issues to the surface — without blame. If blame is the name of the game in your area, problems will stay buried and progress will not be made.
- **Make Growth & Learning a priority.** Encourage everyone to pursue the self-guided QDM training available on the QDM website. Define the core QDM training relevant to your work area.
- **Communicate and celebrate results.** Post metrics and explain their importance to business strategic goals and customer satisfaction. Celebrate QDM results in your formal communications and also informally. This will help build momentum for future improvement.
- **Make time to think creatively.** In addition to tackling nuts-and-bolts improvements, also make time to dream big, think creatively and explore breakthrough ideas such as entirely new processes, products or technologies.

> **Sidebar — "Leading with QDM" Workshop Videos:** This workshop features a set of interactive videos that lead you through the application of QDM principles to real business problems at a hypothetical FedEx company. Search "Leadership Workshop" on the QDM website.

### A Day in the Life of QDM

Every day, QDM is applied in thousands of ways across FedEx. Some typical examples:

- **Monitor the Day's Performance.** From experience, Sam knows that dispatch time is critical to on-time performance in his area. So every day, he creates a run chart for this metric. He finds monitoring helps him identify problems sooner, before they grow.
- **Engage the Frontline.** Ailene, a frontline manager, asks for suggestions. Andrew, a package handler, suggests that the spray bottles for applying wax to a package slide are too small, causing repeated conveyor shutdowns to refill. He suggests a larger size sprayer, available at garden stores. Ailene implements the suggestion and it improves productivity so much that it becomes a standard best practice across the entire OpCo.
- **Know Your Process and Its Root Causes.** Julie, a facility manager, knows that when a problem occurs in her facility's processes, she has to act fast. To help speed response time, she constructs a fishbone diagram showing the frequent causes of quality issues in her area. She posts the diagram in her office initially. But soon, she finds it so useful, she decides to train all team members on the fishbone diagram and how to read it, then posts it in the work area.
- **Take Corrective Action.** A team has had missing shipments in recent days. Using five whys, they discuss and list events that could be the cause. They notice that the battery life of some scanners is short, leading to scan failures. Asking why, they discover that parked vehicles are blocking access to a charging center. The manager takes corrective action to ensure that the vehicles are moved, then orders permanent signage to prevent the issue from recurring.
- **Plan Every Day.** Tyler runs a retail location, and knows that there are certain critical priority projects every day. On arriving at work, he makes a checklist of that day's key tasks. He updates the checklist throughout the day, to stay on track.
- **Collaborating Effectively.** At the end of every shift, Darlene, a frontline manager, puts together a list of key events or issues that happened. She then goes over the list with the manager of the incoming shift to make sure nothing falls through the cracks.
- **Identify Improvement Opportunities.** In the course of gathering call center data, Maria identifies some clear negative trends. To crystallize her thoughts, she drafts opportunity and impact statements, which she shares with her manager. Seeing the impacts, the manager forms a QAT to investigate the best way to address the issues identified.
- **Walk the Work Area.** Kevin, a manager, regularly walks the hangar to identify any safety issues or abnormal conditions — such as tools not being returned to clearly marked storage areas. Kevin knows that, in aircraft maintenance, time is critical and his actions encourage a productive, high-performance workspace for his team.
- **Focus on the Customer Experience.** Customer-facing team members are being locked out of their POS system after entering their password incorrectly multiple times. When this occurs, the team must call Technical Support — while customers wait. The team sees that the customer experience could be improved. So they draft an opportunity statement to share with their manager, who initiates an OpCo-wide QAT based on their insights.

## Quality Metrics

A fundamental principle of quality is to measure what matters most to customers. To help you manage the quality of ongoing business processes, QDM provides for several kinds of standardized measurements — the most important of which is **Service Quality Index (SQI)**.

> **Sidebar — Key Process Indicators (KPIs):** Measurements that are especially relevant to the processes in your own work area. A KPI will often be a metric that influences or drives SQI. For example, at a facility, one KPI might be Vehicle Dispatch Time, because it strongly correlates to the SQI metric On-time Delivery. In this way, KPIs help you better understand the forces that drive customer experience.

### Service Quality Index (SQI)

SQI transforms the first principle of QDM "customers define quality" into real-world, daily measurements that we use to manage our business.

SQI is an integrated set of metrics that are distributed and used enterprisewide to track our daily performance through customers' eyes — and to identify potential quality improvements. SQI is fundamental to how we manage operations.

SQI is scalable, and SQI metrics are defined not only at the enterprise level, but also for each OpCo and business unit. From the top of the enterprise down to the local work group, SQI provides a uniform view of how customers are actually experiencing our performance.

Each metric in the SQI has been carefully researched and standardized to reflect a factor that is important to customers — such as on-time performance, damage claims, sales responsiveness and much more.

The actual composition of SQI is beyond the scope of this guidebook. The table below illustrates the way in which SQI components tie to customer experience.

**SQI: Sample Components**

| Measurement | Definition |
|---|---|
| On Time | Did the shipment arrive when the customer expected it? |
| Shipment Integrity | Did all the pieces of the shipment arrive together? |
| Shipment Condition (Damages) | Did the shipment arrive in good condition? |
| Lost Shipments | Did we know where the shipment was at all times? |
| Tracking Accuracy and Timeliness | Were critical customer-facing scans timely and accurate? |
| Sales Rep Availability | Was a sales representative available when the customer wanted? |
